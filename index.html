AI RESEARCHER

Srivikram MV

I’m interested in how AI systems reason, what makes them more capable, and how we can better understand their behaviour.

I recently completed my MSc at Imperial College London, specialising in AI and machine learning. My research focuses on reasoning models, particularly chain-of-thought faithfulness and monitorability. I've designed controlled benchmarks, built evaluation pipelines, and investigated how models behave when their supplied reasoning conflicts with the task they're solving.

I enjoy taking research questions from an initial observation through to working experiments, analysing the results, and figuring out what to investigate next. My broader interests include model training, reinforcement learning, reasoning, and AI safety. Ultimately, I'd love to contribute to research that makes increasingly capable AI systems more intelligent, reliable, and useful.

My fascination with AI began when I first experienced what language models could do. But what really captured my attention was the emergence of reasoning models.

The idea that a model could spend additional time working through a problem before answering was fascinating to me. And watching how rapidly these models have improved, I've often found myself wondering just how far we can actually take this.

But beyond being amazed by what these models can accomplish, I've become increasingly curious about how they're accomplishing it.

What is it that's making our models so much smarter? Is it the way we train them, the architectures we use, the algorithms behind their reasoning, or something we haven't quite figured out yet? And how do we build on those discoveries to make the next generation of models even better?

That curiosity eventually led me into research. I became particularly interested in chain-of-thought faithfulness and whether the reasoning produced by models actually reflects the processes influencing their answers, as well as what happens when that reasoning conflicts with the problem they're trying to solve.

One thing I've really enjoyed about research is how often an experiment takes me somewhere I didn't originally expect. A result that doesn't quite make sense can turn into an entirely new question, and sometimes that question ends up being more interesting than the one I started with.

My interests aren't limited to understanding existing models, though. I'm equally fascinated by the algorithms and training methods that make modern AI possible, from pre-training and post-training to reinforcement learning and reasoning.

And I don't think progress should be measured only by how many benchmarks a model can solve. I'd love to see AI become genuinely more creative too: capable of writing a beautiful, original novel, making unexpected discoveries, or coming up with ideas that surprise even the people who built it.

I genuinely believe we're still at the beginning of what AI could make possible. I want to spend my career helping push that frontier forward, while contributing to the research needed to make increasingly capable systems safe, reliable, and worthy of our trust.

Correct Answers,
Inaccurate Source Reports

A Controlled Trace-Transfer Study

Can a reasoning model give the correct answer while inaccurately describing the reasoning it was given? I investigate this through controlled trace-transfer experiments, keeping a supplied chain of thought fixed while changing a key fact in the question. The study examines whether models can faithfully report their supplied reasoning, independently of whether they arrive at the correct answer.

One thing I've become particularly curious about is how much we can actually trust what reasoning models tell us about the reasoning they've been given. We often focus on whether a model gets the answer right, but I wanted to investigate whether that tells us anything about how accurately it can describe its supplied chain of thought.

To explore this, I designed an experiment where a model receives reasoning originally written for a slightly different question. By changing the question while keeping the earlier reasoning intact, I could create a controlled conflict between what the model was being asked and what its supplied reasoning actually contained.

What I found particularly interesting was that models could correctly answer the new question while inaccurately reporting details from the original reasoning. Answer correctness and faithful source reporting weren't necessarily the same thing.

For me, that's what makes this distinction worth investigating. If we're going to rely on reasoning traces to help us understand increasingly capable AI systems, we should also understand how reliably models can describe those traces in the first place.

INDEPENDENT RESEARCHIMPERIAL · 2026

Monitoring Conflict Resolution
in Reasoning Language Models

Chain-of-Thought Faithfulness and Prospective Monitoring at the Answer Boundary

I investigate how reasoning models behave when a supplied chain of thought conflicts with the question they're trying to answer. Using a counterfactual benchmark and a correction-aware taxonomy, I examine how models follow, bypass, or correct conflicting reasoning, before exploring whether their forthcoming behaviour can be predicted through repeated sampling, hidden-state representations, and candidate-conditioned likelihoods.

This research began with something that puzzled me while exploring chain-of-thought faithfulness. Existing experiments often approached faithfulness as a relatively binary question: if we change a model's reasoning, does its final answer change too? But when I started experimenting myself, I kept noticing behaviours that didn't fit neatly into that distinction.

That led me to develop a correction-aware taxonomy distinguishing four behaviours: following the supplied reasoning, bypassing it, correcting mistakes within the chain of thought, and transparently correcting them at the final answer stage. I then built a counterfactual benchmark to investigate how different models exhibited these behaviours under controlled reasoning interventions.

What fascinated me next was how the same model could behave differently on two remarkably similar problems. Sometimes it followed the conflicting reasoning; other times it bypassed it. I wanted to understand why, and whether I could anticipate which direction it would take.

That question took the research beyond faithfulness and into prospective monitorability. I explored repeated sampling, hidden-state readouts, and candidate-conditioned likelihood scoring to investigate whether a model's forthcoming behaviour could be predicted before it generated its answer.

On my controlled benchmark, candidate-conditioned likelihood scoring achieved ROC AUCs of 1.000, 1.000, and 0.840 across the three evaluated models.

What I find most interesting is the possibility that even when visible reasoning doesn't reliably tell us what a model is about to do, other signals might. And that opens up an entirely new set of questions I'd love to explore further.

INDEPENDENT RESEARCHNEWCASTLE · 2025

Auditing Fairness
in Large Language Models

Detection and Mitigation Strategies

This research examines bias across several language models and evaluation benchmarks. I built a modular auditing pipeline to compare the different measures, then investigated LoRA fine-tuning and inference-time self-debiasing as mitigation approaches.

This was my first opportunity to take a research question from an initial idea all the way through to designing experiments, implementing them, and interpreting the results.

What interested me was the growing accessibility of smaller, open-weight language models. As more developers begin deploying models locally or within their own infrastructure, I wondered how much we actually know about the biases these models might exhibit, particularly when they're used in applications where fairness matters.

I evaluated several models using a combination of intrinsic and extrinsic bias benchmarks, looking at how biases appeared in their representations and generated responses. I then explored different mitigation strategies, including LoRA fine-tuning and self-debiasing, to see whether those behaviours could be reduced.

Some of the experiments produced encouraging reductions in measured bias, although the results also highlighted how difficult it is to evaluate fairness comprehensively. Improvements on one benchmark don't necessarily mean a model has become fair across every context.

Beyond the findings themselves, I really enjoyed the process of building the evaluation and training pipelines, running experiments, and discovering that the results weren't always what I'd expected. It was one of the experiences that made me realise how much I enjoyed doing research, rather than simply learning about it.
